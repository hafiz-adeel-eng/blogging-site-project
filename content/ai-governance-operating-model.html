THE SHORT ANSWER

An AI governance operating model turns principles into repeatable decisions: who owns a use case, who reviews its evidence, who approves a release, and who responds when the system crosses a boundary. Its essential outputs are decision rights, a use-case register, risk-based release gates, and a tested escalation and fallback process.

This guide is the implementation companion to why AI transformation is a problem of governance. It focuses on operating the process rather than writing a longer policy document.

Separate accountability, specialist review, and operation

Give each use case an accountable business owner. That owner defines the intended outcome and remains responsible for deciding whether the use case should proceed within the organization’s risk policy. Assign an operational owner who can maintain the system, investigate failures, and invoke the fallback.

Security, privacy, legal, data owners, procurement, and affected-user representatives contribute the evidence and decisions appropriate to their remit. Their involvement should not make it ambiguous who is responsible for the overall use case.

Create a use-case record that is useful during an incident

The register should help a responder answer what is running, what it can do, and who can stop it. Avoid collecting fields that no one maintains while omitting the facts needed to intervene.

  • Identity and scope: use-case ID, purpose, users, exclusions, business owner and operational contact.
  • System and data: provider or model label, configuration version, data sources, retention, and access boundaries.
  • Actions: available tools, permissions, permitted autonomous actions, and review conditions.
  • Evidence: evaluations, unresolved findings, release decision, reviewer and decision date.
  • Operation: monitoring, alert owner, stop conditions, fallback and next review trigger.

Record what you cannot observe about a vendor service instead of filling the gap with an assumption. A contract or vendor claim may support due diligence, but it does not replace testing your particular workflow.

Use one intake route and different review paths

A common intake record makes proposals comparable. The review path can then vary according to the consequences. An internal summarization draft may be eligible for an established pattern, while an assistant authorized to change customer entitlements needs a different level of evidence and control.

  1. Register the purpose and accountable owner.
  2. Assess the people, data, actions and downstream consequences.
  3. Choose a predefined control path or escalate an exception.
  4. Collect evidence and record the release decision.
  5. Monitor operation and revisit the decision when a material change occurs.

Use the risk-assessment intake example to distinguish reversible drafting from consequential execution. These internal categories are operational aids, not legal classifications.

Turn permission policy into an enforced boundary

Describe a permission in terms of the operation and resource it authorizes. “May use the CRM” is too broad. “May read these customer-support fields for this authorized user, without updating the record” is testable.

OWASP’s current excessive-agency guidance separates excessive functionality, permissions, and autonomy. A tool with a narrow purpose can still be dangerous if it uses a shared high-privilege account; an approval policy can still fail if the downstream operation does not enforce it. [1]

For each consequential tool, document the authorization check, who can approve the action, and which audit record will show what occurred. Treat a new tool or a broader permission as a potential change to the approved risk profile.

Define material changes before they happen

A material change is one that could alter intended use, affected users, exposure, performance, or the ability to recover. Examples include connecting a new data source, enabling external actions, expanding to a new user group, or changing a core model or evaluation policy.

Not every change must restart the whole process. Reassess the affected controls and record the reasoning. Preserve versions so a responder can identify the configuration associated with a particular outcome.

Review evidence, not document counts

A completed checklist is evidence of a review process, not proof of safety or value. Pair documented controls with tests that could reveal their failure: an unauthorized-user access test, an invalid tool argument, a misleading source, an unrepresented user cohort, or a failed fallback rehearsal.

Microsoft’s Responsible AI Toolbox includes error and fairness analysis that can reveal differences hidden by an overall metric. Use evaluations appropriate to your system; the presence of a dashboard does not establish that its measurements cover your use case. [2]

Run a first review that ends in a decision

Bring one actual use case, its accountable owner, the permitted actions, evaluation results, known uncertainties, and a proposed fallback. End with a recorded decision: approved within scope, approved with explicit conditions, held for evidence, or declined. Assign owners and dates to unresolved work.

Review the program using both workflow outcomes and control effectiveness. A falling review time may be useful if evidence quality is maintained; it is not a success if difficult cases are simply being skipped.

Operating-model questions

Does a small company need an AI governance committee?

Not necessarily. It needs clear accountability, proportionate specialist input, and a route for decisions that exceed routine authority. One person can hold several documented roles when the context allows.

What is the difference between a policy and an operating model?

A policy states expectations and constraints. An operating model defines the people, decisions, evidence, tools, and routines that put those expectations into practice.

When should a use case be reviewed again?

Review it when a defined periodic check is due, a material change occurs, an incident exposes an assumption, or new evidence suggests that the approved controls no longer fit the context.
